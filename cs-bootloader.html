The Context

The moment before everything begins

The bootloader is the first thing a customer ever sees on a Sky device. It runs before the operating system loads, before Sky Glass or EntOS exists from the user's perspective — a narrow window of hardware-level activity that is technically constrained, globally deployed, and permanently baked into the device at the factory.

Until this project, Sky had a dedicated bootloader build tied to its own manufacturing line. It worked for the existing setup but was neither scalable nor future-proof. As Sky moved to expand its hardware partnerships globally — most critically with Rogers in North America — the brittleness of the existing approach became a critical risk.

"The bootloader is built into the hardware at factory level. Whatever we design has to work years from now, on devices we haven't seen yet, in markets we haven't entered."

The Brief

Three problems, one solution

The brief was deceptively simple: design a universal bootloader. In practice, it meant solving three interconnected problems simultaneously — a brand problem, a scale problem and a technical problem — with a solution that had to work in conditions most design work never considers.

01

The brand problem

Sky's new EntOS identity posed a brand encroachment risk with third-party hardware partners. The bootloader could not be overtly tied to Sky's own brand — it needed to feel neutral, or at most, covertly connected for those who knew.

02

The scale problem

The existing build created a dedicated Sky manufacturing line — one build per device variant, one language set per market. Rogers required English and French Canadian simultaneously. The new solution had to support multiple languages and partners from a single build.

03

The technical problem

The bootloader runs under extreme hardware constraints — minimal processing power, no operating system, limited rendering capability. The design had to be simple enough to run on older devices, future-proof enough to ship unchanged for years, and visually coherent across all of them.

My Role

How I contributed

Lead Product Designer

  • Led the design exploration through a Crazy 8s workshop with cross-functional stakeholders
  • Audited competitor bootloader approaches across Apple TV, Apple iOS, Roku, Google TV and Fire TV to define best practice and identify opportunities
  • Developed multiple concept directions ranging from abstracted brand expressions to fully neutral global treatments
  • Balanced the competing constraints of brand neutrality, technical feasibility and global scalability in every design decision
  • Produced final UI assets and specifications for developer handover, with a completion timeline driven by the Rogers critical path

Research

What the competition does

Before designing anything, I audited how the world's major streaming hardware platforms handle the bootloader moment. The patterns were revealing: the best-in-class solutions were minimal, brand-confident and fast. The worst created anxiety through silence or confusion through complexity.

Apple TV Static logo · Progress bar · Static logo · Setup — total ~35s
Apple iOS Static logo · Middleware screen · Setup — total ~18s
Roku Static logo · Animated logo loop · Setup — total ~25s
Google TV Black · Static logo · Animated logo · Black — total ~43s
Fire TV Black · Static logo · Status text · Logo · Animation — total ~3m+

The insight: customers don't need entertainment during a bootloader. They need reassurance. A confident, minimal sequence that communicates "this is working" is more effective than an elaborate animation that risks feeling like something has gone wrong.

Concept Design

Six directions, one workshop

Working with a cross-functional team — Ross, Lorenzo, Hayden, Jack and myself — we ran a Crazy 8s workshop to rapidly generate concept directions before committing to a single approach. Six distinct ideas emerged, each addressing the constraints differently.

Concept 01

EntOS Circle

Leans on the existing EntOS identity but uses it in a way that is abstracted enough to feel neutral to end users, while remaining meaningful to those who know the brand.

Concept 02

RGB Ellipsis

Uses the three primary colours of the light spectrum — which also correspond to the three colours on the remote — to show how Sky forms everything the customer is about to see.

Concept 03

Ellipsis

The ellipsis appears throughout the Sky system to communicate "more". A playful, recognisable treatment that works at any hardware capability level without requiring complex rendering.

Concept 04

Wake

Adapts the existing Sky wake pattern using neutral colours. Since the bootloader runs before the OS wake animation, this would be non-repetitive and give a sense of continuity into the full experience.

Concept 05

Edge

Plays on Sky's Z-axis system transition language, animating in to give the customer a sense of depth and immersion from the very first moment of interaction with the device.

Concept 06

Languages

Embodies Sky's truly global ethos through the use of text in multiple world languages, directly addressing the multi-territory brief and communicating universality from the very first frame.

Impact

What it unlocked

01

A single global build replacing multiple variants

The universal solution replaced a fragmented set of dedicated Sky manufacturing builds with a single bootloader that could ship across any hardware partner, in any market, without modification.

02

Critical path cleared for Rogers launch

The Rogers North America launch — a business-critical milestone requiring English and French Canadian support simultaneously — was unblocked by the new design, which handled multi-language from a single build.

03

Brand-neutral, future-proof

By designing a solution that did not rely on Sky's current brand expression, the bootloader can ship unchanged into new markets and on future hardware without requiring a redesign or a new manufacturing build.

04

Manufacturing and testing efficiencies

Reducing the number of software builds and variants directly reduced manufacturing complexity, cost and testing overhead — a meaningful operational saving at the scale Sky operates.

Reflection

What made this different

Most product design work is reversible. If a UI decision is wrong, you iterate, you push an update, you fix it. The bootloader offered no such safety net. Whatever shipped would be physically embedded in hardware at the factory and could not be changed after the fact.

That constraint forced a different kind of rigour — not just designing something that worked now, but designing something that would still make sense in five years, on hardware that didn't exist yet, in markets we hadn't entered. The Crazy 8s workshop was valuable precisely because it forced the team to explore the full possibility space before committing, rather than defaulting to the obvious Sky brand expression and discovering its limitations later.

The most important design decisions here were the ones we ruled out, not the ones we chose.
